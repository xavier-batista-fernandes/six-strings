Six Strings Guitar theory, one fret at a time
Lesson 1 · Where am I?

The fretboard is a ruler of 12 notes

Music only has 12 different notes. They repeat forever: after B comes C again, just higher. Each fret moves you up exactly one of those notes.

Seven notes get a plain letter: A B C D E F G. The other five live in the gaps and borrow a neighbor's name with a sharp (♯, one fret higher) or a flat (♭, one fret lower). So the note between F and G is called F♯ or G♭. Same fret, two names.

The one rule to memorize: there is no gap between B and C, or between E and F. Everywhere else, natural notes are two frets apart.

Start with the two low strings

Learn the natural notes on the low E and A strings first. Every barre chord has its root on one of those two strings, so once you know them you can find any chord.

Lesson 2 · Distance

Intervals are distances you can see

An interval is the distance between two notes, counted in frets (musicians say "half steps"). One fret is a half step. Two frets is a whole step.

This is the idea that makes guitar easy: scales and chords are just recipes of intervals. "Root, then 4 frets up, then 3 more" is a major chord in every key. Change the starting fret and the recipe still works. That's why your shapes can slide up and down the neck.

You already play one

A power chord is a root plus the note 7 frets above it, the perfect 5th. On guitar that's one string over and two frets up. It's selected on the board below.

Lesson 3 · Note sets

A scale is the set of notes that belong

Out of the 12 notes, a scale picks a handful that sound good together. Solo using only those notes and nothing sounds wrong.

Every scale is a recipe of steps. The major scale is W W H W W W H (sing "do re mi fa sol la ti do"). Start that recipe on any note and you get that note's major scale.

The pentatonic ("five-note") scales drop the two notes most likely to clash. That's why they're the go-to for rock and blues solos. The box you probably already know is shape 1 of the A minor pentatonic, at fret 5. It's shown below.

Lesson 4 · Stacking notes

Chords are built from scales

A chord is three or more notes played together. The basic ones come from the major scale: take the 1st, 3rd and 5th notes. That's it.

Change one note and the chord changes character. Lower the 3rd by one fret and a major chord turns minor. Add the ♭7 and you get the bluesy "7" chord. A chord shape on guitar is just one way to grab those notes with your fingers. Many notes appear twice, in different octaves.

Why E and Em look almost the same

Dots are labelled with intervals. Compare each pair: only the 3 moves, down one fret to ♭3. That one finger is the whole difference between happy and sad.

CAGED: the five shapes behind every chord

Your open chords C, A, G, E and D are the only five shapes you need. Slide any of them up the neck and lay your index finger where the nut used to be. That's a barre chord. An F barre chord is the E shape at fret 1.

Chord
Quality

The shapes link up like puzzle pieces in the order C, A, G, E, D, then start over. Some moved shapes are hard to play in full. Guitarists often grab just three or four strings of them.

Lesson 5 · Families of chords

A key is a home note and its scale

A song "in G" uses the notes of the G major scale, and G feels like home. The chords of the song are built from those same notes.

Build a 1-3-5 chord on each of the 7 scale notes, using only scale notes, and you always get the same pattern: Major, minor, minor, Major, Major, minor, diminished. Musicians number them with Roman numerals, uppercase for major, lowercase for minor. So "a I–IV–V" means the same thing in every key.

Why this helps you

Knowing the key tells you which chords will probably come next, and which scale to solo with. If a song is in G, the G major pentatonic (or its twin, E minor pentatonic) fits over the whole thing.

Relative keys share notes

C major and A minor use exactly the same seven notes. Only the home note differs. Every major key has a minor twin three frets below its root.

The capo trick

A capo moves every shape up together. Capo 2 and play G, C and D shapes, and you're really playing A, D and E. The Roman numerals stay the same.

The V chord pulls home

The V chord (D in the key of G) creates tension that wants to resolve to I. Listen for that pull at the end of the I–IV–V progression above.
